### 1. Executive Summary and Recommendation  

This review focuses on the idea of using Large Language Models (LLMs) to automatically generate LGS-format multiple-choice questions (MCQs) for English reading comprehension and Mathematics. The first paper uses ChatGPT to generate English reading passages and MCQs for middle school students and evaluates these both with automatic metrics and with feedback from students and teachers. The second paper compares four different GPT-4o-based pipelines for generating Mathematics MCQs aligned with the Malaysian curriculum, contrasting prompt-only and RAG-based methods, and paying particular attention to curriculum alignment and reducing hallucinations. In both studies, the core question is not “Can the LLM produce fluent language?” but rather “Are the generated questions truly aligned with learning objectives and pedagogically useful?”

Xiao et al. (2023) compare reading passages and questions generated by ChatGPT for Chinese middle school English with a GPT-2-based baseline and with human-written textbook materials. They report that ChatGPT passages are rated by both students and native evaluators as equal to or even better than human-written texts on many dimensions. However, for multiple-choice questions, especially in terms of distractor quality and question diversity, human-written items still perform better, and teachers emphasize the need to select and filter the model-generated questions. Abdul Wahid et al. (2025) design four pipelines for Form 1 Mathematics: simple and structured prompts plus two RAG approaches (a LangChain-based method and a manual, domain-aware RAG). Their results show that RAG-based methods significantly outperform non-grounded approaches both in semantic similarity to the curriculum (STS) and in content validity as checked by RAG-QA. The first paper is mainly a “classroom-tested, teacher-evaluated application,” while the second paper is much more systematic in terms of “pipeline design and automatic evaluation.”

Taken together, these two studies suggest a two-pronged roadmap for the LGS-LLM project. First, following Xiao et al., we can design an interface where ChatGPT or similar models generate reading passages and question sets, and then test these with teachers. Second, following Abdul Wahid et al., we can build a curriculum-grounded, RAG-based question generation pipeline using Turkish Ministry of Education (MEB) learning outcomes and lesson notes. Both papers show that, with well-designed prompts and appropriate pipelines, LLMs can generate high-quality materials that match students’ proficiency levels. At the same time, there are clear risks: distractors may remain weak, automatic metrics may not fully capture cognitive level or pedagogical suitability, and without teacher oversight the system may mislead students. For this reason, combining RAG-based generation with automatic filtering and explicit teacher-in-the-loop approval looks critical for the design of an LGS-oriented LLM system.

### 2. Annotated Bibliography and Analysis  

**Article 1: Evaluating Reading Comprehension Exercises Generated by LLMs: A Showcase of ChatGPT in Education Applications**  

**Full Citation (APA 7th Style):**  
Xiao, C., Xu, S. X., Zhang, K., Wang, Y., & Xia, L. (2023). *Evaluating reading comprehension exercises generated by LLMs: A showcase of ChatGPT in education applications.* Proceedings of the 18th Workshop on Innovative Use of NLP for Building Educational Applications (BEA 2023), 610–625.  

**In-Text Citation Example:**  (Xiao et al., 2023)  

**Summary of Contribution:**  
This paper introduces a system that uses ChatGPT to generate English reading comprehension exercises for Chinese middle school students. Using a large dataset collected from teachers, the authors compare texts and questions produced by ChatGPT with those produced by a previous model (GPT-2 + PPLM). The reading passages are evaluated with automatic metrics and human raters, while the questions are evaluated only through feedback from teachers and students. The results show that ChatGPT’s passages are in many respects comparable to, or even better than, human-written passages. However, the multiple-choice questions still need improvement, especially in terms of the quality of the distractors.  

**Key Findings & Quotations:**  
- The quality scores of ChatGPT-generated passages are rated by students as higher than human-written texts in all dimensions, including readability, accuracy, coherence, interest, and overall quality.  
- In terms of automatic metrics, ChatGPT passages have a lower Flesch score (harder to read but linguistically richer) and higher lexical diversity (TTR) than human-written texts.  
- For question quality, teachers report that “the questions are well connected to the text, but patterns are repeated too often, some items are too simple, and the distractors are weak.”  

**Personal Analysis and Relevance to LGS-LLM:**  
This study provides a solid pedagogical foundation for the LGS-LLM project. The emphasis on constructing questions that require understanding and reasoning over the text aligns well with the goals of LGS English reading comprehension. However, the system relies only on teacher evaluations and does not integrate AI-based scoring or diagnostic tools. This shows that human judgment is still central, but also that these insights could later be combined with automated evaluation criteria. For our project, the paper highlights that LLMs can reliably generate reading passages, while question and distractor quality still require careful control and likely additional validation steps.

**Article 2: Automated Generation of Curriculum-Aligned Multiple-Choice Questions for Malaysian Secondary Mathematics Using Generative AI**  

**Full Citation (APA 7th Style):**  
Abdul Wahid, R., Nadim, M. S. N., Sulaiman, S., Shaharudin, S. A., Jupikil, M. D., & Jasman Su Azlan Su, I. (2025). *Automated generation of curriculum-aligned multiple-choice questions for Malaysian secondary mathematics using generative AI.*  

**In-Text Citation Example:** (Abdul Wahid et al., 2025)  

**Summary of Contribution:**  
This paper develops four different GPT-4o-based methods for generating mathematics questions aligned with the Malaysian secondary school curriculum. The authors experiment with both simple and structured prompts, as well as RAG-based approaches that incorporate curriculum documents and lesson notes. The generated questions are evaluated with semantic similarity analyses that measure how closely they match the official learning objectives, and with an additional validation step (RAG-QA) that checks whether the questions can actually be answered using the curriculum materials. The results show that RAG-based methods perform much better than prompt-only methods in terms of both curriculum alignment and content correctness.  

**Key Findings & Quotations:**  
- The study emphasizes that “questions generated without grounding may look generally correct, but often do not fully match the content and level required by the curriculum.”  
- In the RAG pipelines, average STS scores are around 0.86–0.89, while in simple prompt-based methods they remain around 0.55–0.58, indicating a substantial difference in curriculum alignment.  
- The authors describe RAG-QA as “a stricter validity measure that tests not just what the question is about, but whether it can actually be answered from the curriculum text.”  

**Personal Analysis and Relevance to LGS-LLM:**  
This paper offers a strong technical framework for the LGS-LLM project. The idea that the model should not generate questions in isolation, but rather be grounded in the official curriculum and lesson materials, is highly relevant for LGS. Such an approach can help ensure that each generated question is explicitly tied to a specific learning outcome. At the same time, the paper shows that automatic metrics are useful as a first filter, but are not sufficient to judge full pedagogical quality, so teacher evaluation remains necessary. Overall, the study suggests that using a RAG-based architecture for LGS Mathematics question generation is likely to produce more accurate, reliable, and curriculum-aligned items.
